\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Small language models are cheap to fine tune and fast to serve, but they
hallucinate, and a hallucination that parses cleanly is invisible to the system
that consumes it. We ask whether the \emph{output format} can be designed to
reduce that risk on a narrow task. We fine tune Qwen3.5-0.8B with LoRA on the
public PIZZA semantic parsing benchmark using six lossless encodings of the same
target tree, arranged as a ladder from \exrTok{} down to \wordsTok{} tokens per
utterance, with two matched token pairs that hold length fixed while varying
surface form. Across \nRuns{} runs we find that shortening the output does
\emph{not} by itself improve accuracy: at 1000 training examples every ten tokens
removed costs about \slopeEmSmall{} points of exact match. What matters is whether the
surface form is made of natural words. A word based encoding matches the native
s-expression on accuracy (\wordsEm{} versus \exrEm{}, inside a measured rerun
noise floor of \noiseFloor{}) while using \tokRatio{}$\times$ fewer tokens,
running \speedup{}$\times$ faster at batch size one, producing
\hallRatio{}$\times$ fewer hallucinated entities, and halving the rate of errors
that parse cleanly but are wrong. We argue this last quantity, which we call the
\emph{silent error rate}, is the right objective for deploying small models. The
matched-pair result replicates on \replLabel{}, whose vocabulary is four times
smaller, where the word based encoding costs \emph{more} tokens than its
symbolic twin and is still the more accurate of the two. We report a café
ordering system built on the finding that runs at 80\,ms per order on a
consumer laptop.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A small language model is attractive for a narrow task. It fine tunes in minutes,
runs on a laptop, and costs almost nothing per call. The objection is
reliability: small models hallucinate~\cite{hallucination}, and in a structured
output setting a hallucination is not an obviously broken string. It is a well formed tree
describing an order nobody placed.

This distinction matters more than overall accuracy. An output that fails to
parse is a \emph{detectable} failure: the caller sees it, rejects it, and retries.
An output that parses but is wrong is a \emph{silent} failure: the caller has no
signal, and a customer receives the wrong order. We therefore separate the two
and treat the silent rate as the quantity to minimise.

Our question is whether the target format itself is a lever. The same semantic
tree can be written many ways, and the choice changes how many tokens the model
must emit, how fast it responds, and apparently how often it invents content.
Our initial hypothesis was simple: fewer output tokens should mean less room to
hallucinate, faster responses, and cheaper fine tuning.

That hypothesis is not supported in the form we posed it. Shortening the output
alone does not improve accuracy, and at low data it clearly hurts. But a sharper
version survives: shortening helps when the surface form stays close to natural
language, and hurts when it becomes symbolic. Section~\ref{sec:results} shows
this with matched token controls that separate length from form.

\textbf{Contributions.}
(1) A ladder of six lossless encodings of the PIZZA target tree spanning
\exrTok{} to \wordsTok{} tokens, with matched token pairs that isolate length
from surface form.
(2) Evidence that length alone is not the mechanism, from \pairdslWordsTotal{}
paired comparisons at equal token budget, all \pairdslWordsSep{} of which
separate in the same direction.
(3) The silent error rate as a deployment objective, with a word based encoding
halving it relative to the native format.
(4) A measured rerun noise floor of \noiseFloor{} exact match, which bounds
what any three seed comparison on this task can claim.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Target representation choice.}
That the choice of meaning representation affects a model's accuracy is not new.
Dong and Lapata~\cite{donglapata} already found that decoding logical forms as
trees rather than flat sequences changed results, and Shin
\emph{et al.}~\cite{shin} made the point closest to ours: prompting a language
model to produce a \emph{canonical natural-language form}, which a grammar then
maps to the meaning representation, beats prompting it for the meaning
representation directly. Our finding is the same phenomenon in a different
regime, and we try to pin down its mechanism. Shin \emph{et al.} work few-shot,
with a frozen model and constrained decoding, where one plausible explanation is
simply that natural-looking strings sit closer to the pretraining distribution a
frozen model can only be steered towards. We fine tune, so the model is given the
chance to learn whatever notation we ask for, and the effect persists. We also
hold token count fixed across a matched pair, which separates naturalness from
length, and we replicate across two subword vocabularies.

\paragraph{Does imposing a format cost accuracy?}
Tam \emph{et al.}~\cite{tam} ask a question adjacent to ours and reach a
compatible answer: restricting a model to JSON, especially through constrained
decoding, degrades performance relative to free-form generation, and not mainly
through parse failures. Read together with our results, the lesson is not that
structure is harmful but that \emph{notation} is the cost. We never relax the
requirement that output be machine parseable; we vary only how it is spelled, and
recover much of the gap at no cost in parseability.

\paragraph{Constrained decoding.}
A large body of work makes invalid output impossible rather than unlikely, by
masking the token distribution against a grammar or automaton:
PICARD~\cite{picard} for SQL, grammar-constrained decoding for structured NLP
tasks generally~\cite{gcd}, and efficient vocabulary indexing to make the mask
cheap~\cite{outlines}. This is complementary to what we study and partly
orthogonal to it. Constrained decoding can drive parse validity to one, which
converts our detectable failures into something else, but it cannot make a
well-formed tree correct. The silent error rate we measure is exactly the part of
the problem constrained decoding does not address, and the format comparison
remains live underneath it: we ship a grammar for the task and report the
unconstrained numbers, because an arm's unconstrained validity is itself
diagnostic of how well the model has internalised the notation.

\paragraph{Hallucination.}
Surveys of hallucination in generation~\cite{hallucination} largely treat it as a
faithfulness problem in open-ended text, where the judgement of what is invented
is itself a modelling problem. A closed-schema parsing task makes the question
decidable: an entity in the output that is absent from the input and from the
schema is invented, and can be counted. We use that to report hallucination as a
rate per utterance rather than as a human judgement.

\paragraph{Tokenization.}
Subword segmentation~\cite{bpe} determines how many tokens a string costs, and
vocabulary fit is known to move downstream accuracy, not just
sequence length~\cite{tokenizer}. Our tokenizer-floor measurement is a narrow
instance: shortening a string stops shortening its token sequence once the string
leaves the vocabulary, which is why our ladder has a floor at about ten tokens,
and why the same format costs different amounts on two different vocabularies.

\section{Task and System}

\subsection{The café parser}
\label{sec:coffee}

The work began with a deployed system. A café ordering booth must turn a spoken
order, already transcribed, into a structured ticket. Orders arrive in Russian
and English, with diminutives, self corrections and off menu items. We fine tuned
Qwen3.5-0.8B with LoRA on a synthetic generator covering ten drinks, three sizes,
five milks and three syrups, with a compact target format:

\begin{center}
\code{cap L oat;lat x2}
\end{center}

where each item is \code{drink [size] [milk] [syrup] [xN]} and \code{?} marks an
utterance that is not an order. On a hand written 49 case evaluation set, written
separately from the generator and containing slang the generator never produces,
the model reaches 48 of 49, emitting about three output tokens per order with a
median latency of 80\,ms. That figure is measured on an Apple M1 through a
local Ollama server using the same Q8\_0 GGUF, which is 795\,MiB on disk. The
compact format uses roughly three times fewer output tokens than an equivalent
JSON encoding, which is most of that latency.

This system works, but it cannot support a claim. The evaluation set is small,
and the same author wrote both the generator and the test cases. To say anything
general we moved to a public benchmark.

\subsection{PIZZA}

PIZZA~\cite{pizza} is a semantic parsing dataset of spoken pizza and drink
orders paired with executable target trees. We use the full published training
pool of \publishedTrainN{} utterances, the 348 utterance development split and
the \nTest{} utterance test split, of which \nPcfg{} are marked as failures of
the authors' PCFG baseline. Our largest training size is \nHeadline{}: the pool
less \publishedGap{} rows whose source and target are identical to an evaluation
utterance. Those rows are dropped from training rather than from the evaluation
splits, so the splits remain exactly as published. Targets look like:

\begin{center}
\footnotesize\ttfamily
(ORDER (PIZZAORDER (NUMBER 2 )\\
(SIZE MEDIUM ) (COMPLEX\_TOPPING\\
(QUANTITY EXTRA ) (TOPPING CHEESE ) ) ) )
\end{center}

We verified that no development or test utterance appears in any training subset
we draw, exactly or after normalisation, at any training size or seed. The check
is on the subsets rather than on the pool because the pool itself is not clean:
it is what turns up the \publishedGap{} rows above.

\section{Output Format Ladder}

Comparing unrelated formats confounds length with surface form. We instead built
six encodings of the \emph{identical} tree that round trip to byte identical
targets, four forming a length ladder and two held at matched length.

\fmt{EXR} is the dataset's native s-expression. \fmt{JSON} is a conventional
structured encoding at nearly identical token count. \fmt{EXR-short} replaces
constructor names with single letters. \fmt{TAGGED} is flat \code{key=value}
pairs. \fmt{DSL} is a compact grammar with sigils, where \code{+} marks an extra
quantity and \code{!} a negation. \fmt{WORDS} shares the DSL's structure but
spells markers out as ordinary words.

All six decode back to the identical tree on every development and test
utterance and on a 50{,}000 example training sample, checked against the
dataset's own matcher.

\paragraph{A tokenizer floor.}
Characters are not tokens, and the gap between them is where the ladder bottoms
out. Figure~\ref{fig:tok} shows one real test utterance, \emph{``\exUtterance{}''},
in all six encodings, with every box a subword produced by the model's own
vocabulary and merge table.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/tokenization}
\caption{The same order in all six encodings. Top: one box per token, all rows to
one scale. Bottom: the matched pair with each box as wide as the characters it

merge table, not drawn.}
\label{fig:tok}
\end{figure}

The bottom row is the floor in miniature. \fmt{DSL} spends three tokens on
\code{+cheese}, since \code{+} is isolated and \code{cheese} after a sigil is
split, where \fmt{WORDS} spends two on \code{extra cheese}. The symbolic form is
shorter as text and longer as tokens. We also built a seventh encoding using
short opaque entity codes, for example \code{p !p5 2 m3 +c2}, which takes the
same effect to its limit: 16.4 characters per utterance against the \fmt{DSL}'s
34.9, less than half, but \emph{more} tokens, 11.9 against 10.8. Shortening the
string stops reducing tokens well before the information content does, which is
why no arm sits below roughly ten tokens and why we did not pursue that arm.

The floor is a property of the subword vocabulary~\cite{bpe,tokenizer}, not of
the format, and the second model measures that directly. Moving from \primaryLabel{}'s \primaryVocab{}
subwords to \replLabel{}'s \replVocab{} costs every arm more tokens, but not
equally: \fmt{JSON} inflates by \infljson{}$\times$ and \fmt{EXR} by
\inflexr{}$\times$, while \fmt{DSL} inflates by \infldsl{}$\times$ and
\fmt{WORDS} by \inflwords{}$\times$. Punctuation-heavy structure is what a
small vocabulary punishes; ordinary words are what it already contains.

\section{Experimental Setup}

Identical across all arms: LoRA~\cite{lora} with $r{=}16$, $\alpha{=}16$, batch 16, cosine
schedule, loss on the completion only. Learning rate is selected per arm from
$\{1\mathrm{e}{-4}, 2\mathrm{e}{-4}, 5\mathrm{e}{-4}\}$ on development exact
match at $n{=}\nLrSelect{}$ across three seeds, then frozen.

The primary model is \primaryLabel{}~\cite{qwen}, with adapters on all attention and MLP
projections and the same prompt template the café model uses. The replication
model is \replLabel{}~\cite{lfm}; Section~\ref{sec:replication} reports it. Three things
about it necessarily differ and we state them rather than implying a matched
setup: ten of its sixteen blocks are convolutional, so there is no equivalent of
an explicit attention projection list and we let the adapter library target every
linear layer, which also reaches the convolution blocks; its prompt comes from its
own chat template, which does not prefill a closed reasoning block as the Qwen
template does; and its token caps are computed separately, because a cap is a
property of the tokenizer. Each model has its own learning-rate sweep, its own
caps and its own noise floor, and the two never share a run directory.
Training uses early stopping on development exact match with patience 10,
evaluated every 100 steps, to a maximum of 4000 steps.

\texttt{max\_new\_tokens} is set per arm to the longest target across all splits
times 1.25, so no gold length output can be truncated. We log the share of
generations reaching that cap; it is zero for every reported run.

Development drives every decision. Test is evaluated once per run and never read
by any selection step. Exact match always comes from the dataset's own unordered
matcher, which we never reimplement.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
Write the test utterances $u_1 \dots u_n$ with gold trees $g_i$, and let $o_i$ be
the model's output and $\pi$ the dataset's parser, with $\pi(o)=\bot$ when $o$ is
not well formed. Let $\simeq$ be the dataset's own unordered tree match. We
report
\begin{align*}
V &= \tfrac{1}{n}\,\bigl|\{\,i : \pi(o_i) \neq \bot\,\}\bigr|
 &&\text{(parse validity)}\\
A &= \tfrac{1}{n}\,\bigl|\{\,i : \pi(o_i) \simeq g_i\,\}\bigr|
 &&\text{(exact match)}
\end{align*}
and split the failures into the two kinds the introduction distinguishes:
\begin{equation*}
\underbrace{A}_{\text{correct}} \;+\;
\underbrace{S}_{\text{silent}} \;+\;
\underbrace{D}_{\text{detectable}} \;=\; 1,
\qquad S = V - A,\quad D = 1 - V .
\end{equation*}
$S$ is the \emph{silent error rate}: output a caller parses successfully and acts
on, that is wrong. $D$ is the share a caller can reject on sight. Figure~\ref{fig:errors}
plots this decomposition for every arm. Note that $A$ alone orders the arms
differently from $S$, which is the practical point: an arm can win on validity
and lose on the errors that actually reach a customer.

\paragraph{Noise floor.}
We retrained one configuration twice under identical settings, per model. Test
exact match differed by \primaryNoise{} on \primaryLabel{} and \replNoise{} on
\replLabel{}. Any difference smaller than the relevant floor is
indistinguishable from rerun variance, and we do not interpret such differences.

\subsection{Shorter is not automatically better}

Table~\ref{tab:main} and Figure~\ref{fig:acc} show no monotone benefit from
shortening. Regressing exact match on mean generated tokens across the four
ladder arms gives a \emph{positive} slope at both training sizes: at $n{=}1000$,
removing ten tokens costs about \slopeEmSmall{} points of exact match.
Compression costs accuracy at both ends of the ladder. \fmt{EXR-short}, which
only renames the native format's constructors, gives up \exrShortDrop{} exact
match against \fmt{EXR} for a quarter of its length, and the worst arm overall is
\fmt{\worstArm{}} at \worstArmEm{}, one of the two shortest. Our original
hypothesis, stated as ``fewer output tokens is better,'' is falsified.

\subsection{Surface form, not length}

The matched token pairs separate the two explanations, under a paired bootstrap
over the \nTest{} test utterances at every pool size and seed. The two pairs do
not behave alike, and the difference between them is the result.

\fmt{DSL} and \fmt{WORDS} differ by less than a token in length, yet \fmt{WORDS}
is ahead in all \pairdslWordsFavB{} of \pairdslWordsTotal{} cells and separates
from the noise in \pairdslWordsSep{} of them. The two encode the same structure;

The \fmt{EXR}/\fmt{JSON} pair is the weaker half of the evidence and we report it

\pairexrJsonTotal{} cells, so the direction is consistent, but only
\pairexrJsonSep{} of them clear the noise floor. That is the expected pattern rather than a disappointment:
both members of this pair are punctuation-heavy symbolic notations, differing in
which brackets they use, so there is little of the natural-word axis between them
to measure. The pair that varies that axis is the pair that separates everywhere.

\subsection{Replication on a second model}
\label{sec:replication}

The result above is the one that carries the argument, so it is the one worth
trying to break. The obvious way it could be an artifact is the tokenizer: if
\fmt{WORDS} wins because the Qwen vocabulary happens to contain the words this
task uses, a model built on a different vocabulary should behave differently. We
therefore repeated the whole grid on \replLabel{}, whose vocabulary is
\replVocab{} against \primaryLabel{}'s \primaryVocab{}, a factor of nearly
four, and whose architecture is largely convolutional rather than attentional.

Three things survive the change of model. The best arm is \replBestArm{} again.
The matched pair keeps both its sign and its size: \fmt{WORDS} over \fmt{DSL}
by \primaryPairGap{} on \primaryLabel{} and \replPairGap{} on \replLabel{},
against rerun noise floors of \primaryNoise{} and \replNoise{}. And the ladder
is non-monotone on both, so the length story fails in the same way twice.

The sign of the token difference is what makes this more than a repetition. Under
\primaryLabel{}, \fmt{WORDS} is marginally the shorter of the pair
(\primaryWordsTok{} against \primaryDslTok{} tokens), so a sceptic can still
attribute its advantage to length. Under \replLabel{} the smaller vocabulary
fragments the word forms more and the ordering inverts: \fmt{WORDS} costs
\replWordsTok{} tokens against \fmt{DSL}'s \replDslTok{}, and it is
\emph{still} the more accurate of the two, by \replPairGap{}. A format that
uses more tokens and makes fewer mistakes cannot be winning because it is short.

We draw no conclusion from the gap between the two models' absolute scores. They
differ in parameter count, in architecture, in which modules carry adapters, and
in whether the prompt prefills a closed reasoning block; any of those could
account for it, and the design separates none of them. The replication is of the
ordering, which is what the ladder and the matched pairs were built to isolate.

Section~\ref{sec:coffee} were taken from. Settling the format question on café
orders needs a larger and harder held-out set, drawn from real transcripts rather
than written by hand; that set does not exist yet.

}{}

\subsection{Silent errors}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/error_composition}
\caption{Where the test set goes for each format, ordered by output length.
Silent errors parse cleanly but are wrong, so a downstream system cannot detect
them. Detectable failures can be rejected and retried.}
\label{fig:errors}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:errors} is the deployment picture. \fmt{EXR} has the highest
parse validity at \exrValid{}, but that is not an unalloyed good: nearly
everything it gets wrong, it gets wrong \emph{silently}, at \exrSilent{}.
\fmt{WORDS} parses less often, \wordsValid{}, but its silent rate is
\wordsSilent{}, a factor of \silentRatio{} lower. Its errors surface as parse
failures a caller can catch.

The hallucination counts move the same way: \wordsHall{} invented entities per
utterance against \exrHall{} for \fmt{EXR}, a factor of \hallRatio{}, and
\wordsMiss{} missing entities against \exrMiss{}.

\subsection{Latency}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/latency_vs_tokens}
\caption{Median latency at batch size one is close to linear in generated
tokens. Throughput in tokens per second is flat across formats, so the speedup
comes from emitting less, not from running faster.}
\label{fig:latency}
\end{figure}

Latency is close to proportional to output length
(Figure~\ref{fig:latency}). \fmt{WORDS} responds in \wordsLat{}\,ms against
\exrLat{}\,ms for \fmt{EXR}, a \speedup{}$\times$ speedup, on a shared GPU under
contention from other tenants. Tokens per second is flat across arms, confirming
the gain comes from length rather than from a faster model.

Memory tells a different story. Across all eighteen measurements the process
holds between \memMin{} and \memMax{}\,MiB, a spread of about fifteen percent
that tracks the longer key value cache the verbose formats need. Weights
dominate, and those are identical across arms. \textbf{Format choice buys
latency and accuracy, not memory.} A practitioner picking a format to fit a
memory budget is picking the wrong lever; the lever for memory is quantisation,
which is what the deployed client in Section~\ref{sec:ipad} uses.

\subsection{Data efficiency}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/data_efficiency}
\caption{Exact match against training set size. The penalty for symbolic formats
is largest when data is scarce, and narrows as data grows.}
\label{fig:data}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:data} shows the format penalty is a low data phenomenon. At
$n{=}1000$, \fmt{DSL} reaches \dslEmSmall{} against \fmt{WORDS} at
\wordsEmSmall{}. By $n{=}\nHeadline{}$ the gap narrows. A format closer to natural
language appears to let the model reuse what pretraining already gave it, which
matters most when there is little task data to learn from.

\subsection{Deployment on device}
\label{sec:ipad}

The café parser runs entirely on an iPad, with no network call. The client is a
SwiftUI application that links llama.cpp as an \texttt{xcframework} and offloads
every layer to the Metal GPU. Table~\ref{tab:device} lists what it costs to
carry.

The spread between the median and the p90 is itself the paper's argument,
restated on the hardware a customer waits in front of. Across the ten orders the
benchmark parses, latency is close to linear in the number of output tokens:
a least squares fit gives 48\,ms of fixed overhead plus 52\,ms per generated
token ($R^2 = 0.91$), so a one token reply such as \code{esp} returns in about
100\,ms while the longest, a seven token two drink order, takes about 414\,ms.
The format's token count is therefore not a throughput abstraction but the
dominant term in the wait, and the \tokRatio{}$\times$ reduction the word based
encoding buys becomes the same factor of responsiveness at the booth.

The footprint deserves a note, because \padMem{} is well under the 795\,MiB
model file. The figure is \texttt{phys\_footprint}, which is what iOS enforces
its per-process memory limit against, and llama.cpp maps the weight file rather
than copying it, so the weight pages are file backed and are reclaimable under
pressure instead of counting against that limit. The number to plan a deployment
around is therefore the resident figure, not the file size, and on this device it
leaves the parser a long way inside the budget of even an entry level iPad.

Two further details matter for reproducibility. First, the prompt template and
the three stop strings compiled into the client are byte identical to those used
for training and for every evaluation in this paper, so the deployed model sees
exactly the input distribution it was fitted on. Second, the context window is
512 tokens, which the compact format fits comfortably and the equivalent JSON
encoding would not at the longest orders. A format chosen for token count at
training time therefore also decides whether the model fits a small on-device
context at serving time.

The practical consequence is that a booth needs no server, no network round
trip, and no per-request cost. The whole parser is a 795\,MiB file beside an
11\,MiB runtime.

\subsection{Reproducibility}

Every number in this paper is recomputed from saved model outputs rather than
copied from a training log. Each model has its own grid of \nRuns{} runs under
its own directory, so no token cap, learning rate or noise floor is ever shared
between them. Each run writes its configuration, package versions, training
curves, adapter weights and the full prediction file for both evaluation
splits, together with the generation contract it used: padding side, stop token
and id, vocabulary size and the resolved prompt. We record the last of these
because an earlier replication grid was run with right padding, which places
pad tokens between the prompt and the continuation, and scored between 0.13 and
0.20 lower on every arm; those runs were discarded and rerun, and the arm
ordering was unaffected. A prediction row carries the source utterance, the
gold tree, the raw model output, the parsed tree or a null, a correctness flag
and the generated token count, so exact match can be recomputed offline with no
GPU. Every table and figure here is produced by two scripts that read only those
files. The protocol, including the stopping rules and the statements this work
declines to make, was written before any run and records its own later
amendments.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{Which format should a practitioner choose?}
\fmt{WORDS}. On \primaryLabel{} it ties the native format on accuracy within the
noise floor and wins on every deployment axis: \tokRatio{}$\times$ fewer tokens,
\speedup{}$\times$ lower latency, \hallRatio{}$\times$ fewer hallucinations, and
\silentRatio{}$\times$ fewer silent errors. It is also the best arm on
\replLabel{}, by a margin outside that model's own noise floor, so the
recommendation does not rest on one tokenizer. The recipe is to compress the
\emph{structure} while keeping the \emph{vocabulary} natural.

\paragraph{How small can the model be?}
At \nHeadline{} training examples \primaryLabel{} reaches \primaryWordsEm{}
exact match on \fmt{WORDS}. \replLabel{}, less than half the parameters,
reaches \replWordsEm{} on the same arm.

This is now a like-for-like comparison on data, which earlier versions of this
study could not make. The published \publishedSystem{} result on this benchmark
is \publishedEm{}, trained on \publishedTrainN{} utterances; our headline grid
trains on \nHeadline{}, the same pool less the \publishedGap{} rows that
duplicate an evaluation utterance and that we drop as contamination. At that
budget \primaryLabel{} is \headlineBeatsPublished{} ahead on its best arm. We
still decline a state-of-the-art claim, but the reason is now narrower than it
was: not the data budget, which is matched, but the architecture and training
regime. \publishedSystem{} is a fully fine tuned encoder-decoder; this is a
decoder-only model of a different size carrying LoRA adapters, and neither
design was tuned to compete with the other. The number is a reference line, and
what we read off it is only that a sub-billion-parameter model with adapters is
in the same range as the published baseline once the format is chosen well.

What is worth noting is the size of the format effect against the size of the
model effect. Choosing the format moves exact match by \primaryArmSpread{} across
the six arms on \primaryLabel{} and \replArmSpread{} on \replLabel{}, while the
two models differ by \modelGapBest{} on their best arm. On the smaller model the
format matters more than the choice of model; on the larger one the two effects
are comparable. Either way, format is not a rounding error next to capacity.
Both sizes serve in real time on consumer hardware, which the café system in
Section~\ref{sec:coffee} does at 80\,ms per order.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
One task that resolves the question and two models, neither of them large. The
replication in Section~\ref{sec:replication} shows the ordering survives a
fourfold change of vocabulary, but both models are under a billion parameters and
both grids are the same 1{,}357 utterance test split, so the finding is not
established beyond this task.%
% Guarded on the same file as the section itself: without the cafe grid the label
% does not exist and the reference would render as a question mark.
\IfFileExists{generated/coffee.tex}{ The second task we ran, in
Section~\ref{sec:coffee-results}, was saturated and separates nothing in either
direction; it bounds the claim rather than supporting it.}{} The replication was also added after the primary grid rather than
pre-registered, and is reported as such. Three seeds per cell gives wide
intervals, which is why arm comparisons use a paired bootstrap over utterances
rather than seed means.
Learning rate is tuned at $n{=}\nLrSelect{}$ and reused at every pool size. Latency is
measured on a contended shared GPU, so absolute milliseconds are inflated,
though every arm meets the same contention. We have not tested whether these
findings transfer to a larger model, where the format penalty may vanish.
Finally, the two models differ in capacity, architecture, adapter placement and
prompt conditioning at once, so nothing here attributes any difference between
their absolute scores to any one of those.

\section{Conclusion}

Output format is a free design variable that changes hallucination rate and
latency by factors of three to four. The intuition that fewer tokens is safer is
not quite right. What helps is compressing structure while keeping the surface
in natural words, and what matters for deployment is not overall accuracy but
how often a model is wrong in a way nobody can detect. The ordering survives a
fourfold change of subword vocabulary, and on the smaller vocabulary the word
based format costs more tokens than its symbolic twin while remaining more
accurate, which is the clearest evidence we have that the surface form and not
the length is doing the work.
